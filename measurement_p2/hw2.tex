\subsection{1. Sampling (1 points)}\label{sampling-1-points}

\textbf{(a) How should you transform pweight to obtain a weight that
allows you to estimate statistics on the population of women in Cote
d'Ivoire? Explain your computations.}

I can use the pweight column to balance the class distribution of the
sample to match the population distribution. The \texttt{pweight} column
contains sample weights that modify the contribution of each observation
to the overall statistics. It works as a frequency multiplier, where
each observation is counted as if it were \texttt{pweight} number of
observations. In practice, R can use the pweight column to compute
weighted statistics, such as means and proportions by using the
\texttt{weights} argument in functions like \texttt{lm()} or
\texttt{survey::svydesign()}. Internally, weighted functions such as
weighted means and lm will multiply each observation by its
corresponding weight. For example a weighted mean will be computed using
equation Equation~\ref{eq-weighted_mean}, and weighted linear regression
will minimize the weighted sum of squared residuals, as shown in
equation Equation~\ref{eq-weighted_lm}.

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-weighted_mean}{
\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i}
}\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-weighted_lm}{
\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
}\end{equation}

\subsection{2. Measurement of under 5 mortality rate (5
points)}\label{measurement-of-under-5-mortality-rate-5-points}

\textbf{(a) What method can be deemed preferable and why? Give the pros
and cons of both methods.}

The preferable method is method 2 (M2) because method 1 (M1)
underestimates the mortality rate by not capturing the deaths that occur
after the survey period. Figure~\ref{fig-mortality} shows graphycally
the surveyed groups: the x-axis represents the age of the child at the
time of the survey, and the y-axis represents the age of the child at
the time of death. The red arrow at 45 degrees indicates the frontier of
possible outcomes, since by definition noone can die after their current
age, the survey caputures outcomes below the frontier. Notice M1
captures area B and drops area C underestimating the true mortality
rate. M2 captures area C which represents the true mortality rate. For
example a surveyed mother has a child one year before the survey and the
child dies the year after the survey (area C in
Figure~\ref{fig-mortality}), M1 will not capture his death because by
definition nothing can die after its current age.

M1

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Pros: it is easy to compute, captures the most recent deaths
  (recency).
\item
  Cons: underestimates the mortality rate, downward biased, does not
  capture deaths after the survey period.
\end{itemize}

M2

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Pros: provides a robust estimate of the true mortality rate.
\item
  Cons: it requires a longer period of time to capture deaths, less
  recent than M1.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Stunted children are defined as children showing a height stature
that is lower than the median of the reference population by 2 standard
deviations measured on the same reference population. That is children
such as the z-score is lower than -2 (}\(z \le -2\)), where the z-score
is given by the following formula:

\[Z = \frac{\text{height} - \text{WHOMedian}(\text{sex}, \text{age})}{\text{WHOstdev}(\text{sex}, \text{age})}\]

\subsection{1. Weight Unit Correction}\label{weight-unit-correction}

\emph{In 2008, the weight could be measured in either kilograms or
hectograms. However, the unit was not systematically reported. It was
therefore assumed that all the weights were reported in kilograms.}

\textbf{(a) Derive an expression for the sample mean weight in this
context, and show that, if we knew just one parameter, we could obtain a
non-biased estimate of the mean weight despite the hectogram issue. (.5
point)}

Let's denote the estimated mean weight as \(W_{\text{obs}}\), the true
mean weight as \(W_{\text{true}}\), and the proportion of weights
measured in hectograms as \(p_h\). The observed mean weight can be
expressed as the sum of the mean weights measured in kg and in
hectograms multiplied by their corresponding probabilities:

\[
\begin{aligned}
E[W_{\text{obs}}] &= (1 - p_h) \cdot W_{\text{true}} + p_h \cdot W_{\text{true}}\cdot 10\\
  &= W_{\text{true}} \cdot (1 - p_h + 10 p_h)\\
  &= W_{\text{true}} \cdot (1 + 9 p_h)
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus, having an estimate of \(p_h\) would allow us to correct the
observed mean weight. Notice that if \(p_h = 0\), then
\(E[W_{\text{obs}}] = W_{\text{true}}\), and if \(p_h = 1\), then
\(E[W_{\text{obs}}] = 10 W_{\text{true}}\).

For applying the correction, it is necessary to have a good estimate of
\(p_h\). In this case, it can be approximated by the proportion of
weights above 25kg in 2008 which is 30.4\% of the observations. Using
this, we can correct the mean weight estimate by dividing the observed
mean weight by \((1 + 9 p_h)\). See \ref{tbl-corrected_weight_mean}
where the 2008 mean weight is corrected. After correction, the mean
weight in 2008 is \(11.48\)kg, which is consistent with previous years.

As shown in Table \ref{tbl-stunted_north_south}, the national stunting
rate for 2-4 years old children with outliers is 24.1\% in 1988, 42\% in
1993, and 46.4\% in 2008. This increasing trend shows an overall
increment of 22.3 percentage points in the stunting rate over the
20-year period.

The north and south regions an increasing trend overtime, but the north
region shows a higher stunting rate than the south region in 1988 and
2008. Particularly, in 1988 the difference between north and south is
5.6 percentage points at the 1\% significance level, and in 2008 the
difference is 4.5 percentage points at the 5\% significance level. In
1993 the difference is not statistically significant different frm zero,
indicating that the north and south regions had similar stunting rates
in 1993. Many theories can explain the differences between north and
south e.g.~differences in measurement, differences in the population, or
the effect of socioeconomic factors such as poverty-alleviating
programs, and access to health care.

however, in 2008 the stuting is not as different from 1933 except in the
north region where there is a difference of 4 percentage points. This
change in the pattern suggest that the outliers correction had an impact
on the results and drafted conclusions. Regional differences between
north and south persist, there are still a significant difference of 6.1
and 3.5 percentage points in 1988 and 2008 at the 1\% and 10\%
significance levels respectively between the north and south regions
despite the change in the stunting rate over time pattern.

girls is increasing over time for the model with outliers (LHS four
models). Starting from 1988, boys and girls had a stunting rate of
24.6\% and 23.6\% with no significant difference. In 1993, boys and
girls had a stunting rate of 43\% and 40\% with a significant difference
of 3 percentage points indicating boys had a higher stunting rate. In
2008, boys and girls had a stunting rate of 47\% and 45\% respectively
with no significant difference.

in 2008. In this period, removing the outliers lowered the national
stunting rates from 47\% with outliers to 41\% without outliers.
Differences between boys and girls are significant at 5\% significance
level in 1993, while there is no significant difference in 1988 and
2008.

Observations are confirmed by Figure
\ref{fig-distributions_stunted_male_female} where the distribution of
z-scores less than -2 is higher for boys than girls in 1993, while in
1988 and 2008 the distributions are similar.

\subsection{4. Height Stature and poverty (2
points)}\label{height-stature-and-poverty-2-points}

\textbf{Here, one would like to study how the gradient of the number of
stunted children aged 2-4 years, and of household consumption per
capita, as well as the relationship between the two, have changed over
time.} \textbf{Find a graphical or regression framework to represent
this (many options are possible). Comment on your results and their
limits.}

Figure \ref{fig-over_time} represents the relationship between the log
of household consumption per capita and the probability of being stunted
for 2-4 years old children by year. The figure was created by binning
the log of household consumption per capita into ventiles and
calculating the weighted stunting rate for each ventile. Then a linear
regression line was fitted to the binned data for each year.

Interpreting the results in Figure \ref{fig-over_time}, there is a clear
trend increasing the stunting rate across years explained by the
vertical shift of the regression lines. The slope of each regression
line is negative, indicating that higher household consumption per
capita is associated with a lower probability of being stunted. While
the regression lines in 1988 and 2008 have similar slopes, the
regression line in 1993 is steeper, indicating that the socioeconomic
gradient of stunting was more pronounced in 1993 than in 1988 and 2008.

Analytically, the results can be confirmed through the linear regression
model in @eq:over\_time\_model. Where
\(log(\tilde{\text{Conspc88}_i}))\) is the log of household consumption
per capita centered around its mean, \(\beta_0\) is the stunting rate
for the mean child household consumption, and \(\beta_1\) is the effect
of log household consumption on the probability of being stunted in
1988. \(\beta_2\) and \(\beta_3\) capture the change in the stunting
rate in 1993 and 2008 relative to 1988, while \(\beta_4\) and
\(\beta_5\) capture the change in the effect of log household
consumption on the probability of being stunted in 1993 and 2008
relative to 1988. \(Z_i\) is a vector of control variables including
sex, age, and education. The results for all population and for each
tercile are summarized in Table \ref{tbl-stunting_by_income}.

\[
\begin{aligned}
Stunted_i & = \beta_0 + \beta_1 log(\tilde{\text{Conspc88}_i}) + \beta_2 Y93_i + \beta_3 Y08_i + (\beta_4 Y93_i \times log(\tilde{\text{Conspc88}_i})) + \beta_5 (Y08_i \times log(\tilde{\text{Conspc88}_i})) + Z_i + \epsilon_i\\\\
& \text{where } \tilde{\text{Conspc88}_i} = log(\text{Conspc88}_i) - \overline{log(\text{Conspc88})}
\end{aligned}
\] \{\#eq:over\_time\_model\}
